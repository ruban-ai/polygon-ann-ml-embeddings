\begin{abstract}
Similarity search over polygonal datasets is a critical spatial-data operation, with Weighted Jaccard (WJ) capturing area-based shape overlap. The current state-of-the-art ShapeToVec encodes a polygon leveraging a quad-tree-based non-uniform grid, accounting for extreme area variations, employing a Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) index for fast retrieval. However, their dimensionality imposes a heavy memory cost and query throughput penalty. We propose an efficient learned compact WJ-compatible compression employing a simplex-constrained encoder, giving $36\times$ smaller vectors.
On a 10k subset of the Parks dataset, a WJ-native triplet model with false-negative filtering and decoder reconstruction reaches Recall@50 $=0.858$ at 10{,}903 QPS (78$\times$ faster than brute-force Improved Consistent Weighted Sampling) and Recall@10 $>0.996$ after exact-WJ reranking.
At the full 187k-polygon scale, however, we find that ranking-style objectives (triplet, InfoNCE) no longer beat an untrained random projection: recall is governed by faithful Weighted-Jaccard \emph{rank preservation}, which a separation proxy does not optimize, and the contrastive loss in fact damages the very output layer that is deployed. We close this gap with \textbf{WJ-native distillation}: regressing the embedding's Weighted Jaccard directly onto the true Weighted Jaccard aligns the training objective with the retrieval metric, and a single Matryoshka encoder then serves every dimension. Distillation is the strongest method at \emph{both} scales --- at 10k it reaches Recall@50 $=0.930$ (above even brute-force ICWS) at 14{,}552 QPS, and at the full 187k scale, at just 256 dimensions, it is --- to our knowledge --- the first learned WJ embedding to surpass random projection at both near- and wide-net recall: Stage-1 Recall@500 of 0.886 versus 0.722, and 0.964 versus 0.927 after exact reranking, at $\sim$2$\times$ the throughput.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Geospatial applications increasingly rely on large polygon datasets --- parks,
water bodies, parcel boundaries, and land-use regions --- where finding similar
shapes is essential for data deduplication, conflation, and spatial analytics.
Weighted Jaccard (WJ) similarity,
\begin{equation}
  WJ(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = \frac{\sum_i \min(a_i,b_i)}{\sum_i \max(a_i,b_i)},
  \quad \mathbf{a},\mathbf{b} \geq \mathbf{0},
  \label{eq:wj}
\end{equation}
captures area-weighted shape overlap and is the standard metric for this task.
A central obstacle in encoding such polygons is \emph{extreme area variability}:
in real-world GIS corpora, polygon areas span several orders of magnitude (e.g.\
a neighbourhood park versus a national forest). A uniform rasterisation grid
cannot serve both regimes --- a grid fine enough to resolve small polygons
explodes in dimensionality, while a grid coarse enough to be tractable leaves
small polygons occupying only a handful of cells, collapsing their shape
information into near-empty vectors. ShapeToVec~\cite{shape2vec} resolves this
with an adaptive \emph{quadtree} grid that recursively subdivides space where
polygon detail is concentrated, encoding each polygon as an 18{,}219-dimensional
probability-simplex vector whose WJ preserves area-weighted shape overlap across
all scales. Indexed directly with HNSW in nmslib's
\texttt{WeightedJaccard} space~\cite{shape2vec}, ShapeToVec attains 97\% R@50 on corpora
up to 1.7M polygons. However, indexing such large vectors becomes increasingly
expensive as corpora grow; compressing them while preserving WJ structure
enables faster search and lower memory overhead at scale.

Existing approaches do not fill this need (\S\ref{sec:related}): classical WJ
sketches (ICWS~\cite{ioffe10}, ProbMinHash~\cite{probminhash20},
PolyMinHash~\cite{polyminhash25}) output integer codes needing brute-force or
LSH-band search, while learned hashing and metric
learning~\cite{hashsurvey14,deephashsurvey22} target only Euclidean or Hamming
spaces --- none produce WJ-compatible embeddings.

We address this gap. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{WJ-native embedding}: ReLU\,+\,$\ell_1$ outputs keep embeddings on
    the probability simplex, so WJ is computed directly in nmslib's
    \texttt{WeightedJaccard} HNSW space~\cite{shape2vec} with no post-processing.
  \item \textbf{WJ-native metric learning}: a three-layer MLP trained with a WJ
    triplet loss and false-negative filtering substantially outperforms PCA, NMF,
    and random projection (Table~\ref{tab:results}); a decoder reconstruction term
    adds a further 5.0\,pp R@50 --- but only with filtering active, else
    conflicting gradients collapse recall to 0.510.
  \item \textbf{Objective--metric alignment scales where ranking does not.}
    On the full 187k-polygon corpus, ranking objectives (triplet, InfoNCE) degrade
    to the level of an untrained random projection --- not for lack of capacity, but
    because recall is governed by Weighted-Jaccard \emph{rank preservation} rather
    than pairwise separation, and the contrastive loss damages the deployed output
    layer itself (\S\ref{sec:fullscale}). Training the encoder by
    \emph{WJ-native distillation} --- regressing its WJ onto the true WJ --- aligns
    the objective with the metric, yielding a single \emph{Matryoshka} encoder that
    at 256 dimensions surpasses random projection at both near- and wide-net recall
    (Stage-1 R@500 0.886 vs.\ 0.722; reranked 0.964 vs.\ 0.927) at $\sim$2$\times$
    throughput --- the first learned WJ embedding to do so at scale.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Polygon representation and ShapeToVec.}
ShapeToVec~\cite{shape2vec} encodes polygons with an adaptive quadtree
rasterisation that handles extreme area variation (Sec.~\ref{sec:intro}),
indexing the resulting 18{,}219-dimensional WJ simplex vectors with
HNSW~\cite{hnsw20} in nmslib's \texttt{WeightedJaccard} space~\cite{shape2vec}
to reach $\geq$97\% R@50 on corpora up to 1.7M polygons. Our
work compresses these vectors up to $71\times$ while preserving their WJ structure.

\paragraph{WJ sketching.}
ICWS~\cite{ioffe10} generates 512-dimensional sketches where the expected number
of agreeing positions equals the WJ similarity, providing an unbiased estimator.
ProbMinHash~\cite{probminhash20} accelerates sketch generation by orders of
magnitude while preserving the same guarantee.
Both methods output integer codes incompatible with float-vector approximate nearest
neighbor (ANN) indexes, requiring brute-force or LSH-band search at query time.
PolyMinHash~\cite{polyminhash25} applies area-based MinHashing directly to polygon
geometry with LSH banding for candidate pruning, but is non-learned.

\paragraph{Learned hashing, metric learning, and distillation.}
Learning-to-hash~\cite{hashsurvey14,deephashsurvey22} and metric-learning methods
produce binary or real-valued embeddings for Hamming/cosine ANN search; Matryoshka
Representation Learning~\cite{matryoshka22} adds nested truncation but, being
cosine-space, degrades on the WJ simplex (Table~\ref{tab:results}).
NMF~\cite{nmf99} yields label-free simplex-compatible factors but optimises
reconstruction, not WJ retrieval. Knowledge distillation~\cite{hinton15} transfers
a teacher's outputs to a student; we adapt this idea to a \emph{metric}, distilling
the exact Weighted Jaccard into a compact simplex embedding. None of the above
target WJ as the native metric.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Given a corpus $\mathcal{D} = \{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^N$ of ShapeToVec polygon
encodings $\mathbf{x}_i \in \Delta^D$ ($D = 18{,}219$) and a set of ground-truth
WJ nearest-neighbour pairs $\mathcal{P} \subset \mathcal{D} \times \mathcal{D}$,
our goal is to learn a compact encoder $f_\theta: \Delta^D \to \Delta^d$ ($d \le 512$)
such that WJ similarity is preserved in the compressed space, enabling fast HNSW
retrieval with WJ as the index metric.

\subsection{Encoder Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

The encoder $h_\theta$ is a three-layer MLP with Batch Normalization (BN) and
ReLU activations ($18{,}219 \to 4{,}096 \to 1{,}024 \to d$, $\sim$80M
parameters). Three considerations dictate this design, each driven by the
structure of WJ and of the quadtree input rather than by convention.

\noindent\textbf{Why an MLP, not a Transformer or graph encoder.}
The neural polygon encoders common in the literature --- boundary
Transformers~\cite{mai22}, vertex graph networks~\cite{polygongnn24}, and set
encoders~\cite{deepsets17} --- target cosine or Euclidean output spaces and are
ill-suited to WJ retrieval. Two mismatches explain this. First,
\emph{space mismatch}: an encoder trained under an L2 or cosine objective learns
a metric structure that is destroyed when its outputs are forced onto the WJ
simplex via ReLU\,+\,$\ell_1$-normalisation. Second, \emph{loss of global
structure}: sequence- and graph-based encoders aggregate \emph{local} context
(token windows, vertex hops), whereas WJ is a global statistic summed over all
$D$ dimensions that cannot be decomposed into local sub-computations. A plain
MLP trained \emph{natively} in WJ space avoids both.

\noindent\textbf{Global projection.}
Accordingly, the first layer is a single dense projection
$\mathbb{R}^{18{,}219}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}^{4{,}096}$ that mixes \emph{all} input
dimensions in one matrix multiply, so every pair of quadtree cells can interact
and the cross-dimension co-occurrence statistics WJ depends on are preserved.
This global mixing is why $\sim$75M of the $\sim$80M parameters reside in this
first layer.

\noindent\textbf{BatchNorm before ReLU.}
Quadtree rasterization yields extremely sparse vectors (mean $\sim$73\% zeros,
mean non-zero entry $\sim$$10^{-9}$), so pre-activation responses have near-zero
variance. Applying ReLU directly would suppress almost all activations and
produce a degenerate output whose pairwise WJ varies by $\sim$$10^{-4}$ --- too
little to rank candidates. Placing BN \emph{before} each ReLU re-centers and
rescales the activations first, guaranteeing a non-degenerate output distribution.

\subsection{Probability Simplex Output Constraint}

WJ (Eq.~\ref{eq:wj}) requires non-negative inputs. We enforce this with a final
simplex projection layer:
\begin{equation}
  f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\operatorname{ReLU}(h_\theta(\mathbf{x}))}
                              {\max\!\bigl(\|\operatorname{ReLU}(h_\theta(\mathbf{x}))\|_1,\;\epsilon\bigr)}.
  \label{eq:simplex}
\end{equation}
The output sequence BN\,$\to$\,ReLU\,$\to$\,$\ell_1$-norm is necessary and
sufficient for WJ compatibility: non-negativity makes the element-wise
$\min$/$\max$ meaningful, and $\ell_1$-normalisation places every embedding on
the probability simplex $\Delta^{d}$ so the $\min$/$\max$ ratio is computed at
a comparable scale. Together this (i)~makes
$WJ(f_\theta(\mathbf{x}), f_\theta(\mathbf{y}))$ well-defined for any input pair,
and (ii)~enables direct use of nmslib's \texttt{WeightedJaccard} HNSW
space~\cite{shape2vec} without post-processing. Every objective below
(Secs.~\ref{sec:triplet}--\ref{sec:distill}) operates through this same output.

\subsection{WJ Triplet Loss with False-Negative Filtering}
\label{sec:triplet}

Training uses an \emph{anchor-positive} dataset of GT pairs
$\{(\mathbf{a}_i, \mathbf{p}_i)\}_{i=1}^B$ per batch.
Hard negatives are mined in-batch via the $B \times B$ cross-WJ similarity matrix
$S_{ij} = WJ(f_\theta(\mathbf{a}_i), f_\theta(\mathbf{p}_j))$.
The hard negative for anchor $i$ is:
\begin{equation}
  j^* = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{j \neq i,\, (i,j) \notin \mathcal{G}} S_{ij},
  \label{eq:hardneg}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{G} = \{(i,j) : j \text{ is a GT neighbour of } i\}$ is the
precomputed set of ground-truth neighbour pairs --- the \emph{false-negative (FN)
filter}.
Without this filter, GT neighbours appearing in the batch are treated as negatives,
creating gradients that push WJ-similar pairs apart.
The triplet loss over violated pairs $\mathcal{V} = \{i : \text{loss}_i > 0\}$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{trip}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}
    \sum_{i \in \mathcal{V}} \bigl[WJ(\mathbf{n}_i, \mathbf{a}_i)
    - WJ(\mathbf{p}_i, \mathbf{a}_i) + m\bigr]_{+},
  \label{eq:triplet}
\end{equation}
with margin $m = 0.3$, where $\mathbf{n}_i = f_\theta(\mathbf{p}_{j^*})$.

\subsection{Reconstruction-Augmented Autoencoder}
\label{sec:ae}

The triplet loss teaches only coarse rank order. We add a decoder
$g_\phi: \Delta^d \to \mathbb{R}^D_{\geq 0}$ (three-layer MLP), forming an
autoencoder, with reconstruction loss
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}} = \tfrac{1}{2}(
  \operatorname{MSE}(g_\phi(f_\theta(\mathbf{a})), \mathbf{a}) +
  \operatorname{MSE}(g_\phi(f_\theta(\mathbf{p})), \mathbf{p}))$,
and train $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{trip}} +
\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}$ ($\lambda{=}0.1$).
Reconstruction forces the encoder to preserve the full input distribution, not
just rank order, improving WJ discrimination --- but only with FN filtering
active: otherwise it anchors positives at high WJ while corrupted triplet
gradients push in-batch false negatives below the margin, a conflict that
collapses recall (Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}). This model gives our best
\emph{moderate-scale} results (Table~\ref{tab:results}); the next two sections
show why it --- and ranking objectives in general --- stops being enough at scale.

\subsection{Why Ranking Objectives Misalign at Scale}
\label{sec:filter}

Every encoder above serves the same role: a Stage-1 \emph{candidate generator}
whose top-$K$ output is reranked exactly in Stage~2. What matters is therefore not
the order it produces but \emph{which} polygons reach the pool, which in turn
depends on how faithfully the embedding preserves the true WJ \emph{ranking} of a
query's neighbourhood. The triplet objective (Sec.~\ref{sec:triplet}) is instead a
\emph{separation proxy}: it pushes the single hardest in-batch negative below the
positive by a margin and saturates once that margin is met. As the corpus grows,
that hardest negative is drawn from a vanishing fraction of the data, the margin is
satisfied by coarse separation, and the generator preserves only the immediate
neighbourhood --- recall stops improving with $k$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:fullscale}).
A non-saturating in-batch InfoNCE over WJ similarity (every step contributes
gradient, supervising the broad top-$P$ neighbourhood) is \emph{partially} aligned
and recovers wide-net recall, but it is still a separation proxy and, like the
triplet, leaves the deployed layer distorted (Sec.~\ref{sec:fullscale}). This
motivates supervising the embedding with the metric \emph{itself}.

\subsection{WJ-Native Distillation}
\label{sec:distill}

We align the objective with the retrieval metric directly. For a batch of $M$
encodings we form the true pairwise WJ on the original $D$-dimensional vectors,
$T_{ij}=WJ(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)$, and the embedding WJ,
$\hat T_{ij}=WJ(f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i),f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_j))$, and minimise
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}} = \frac{1}{M^2}\sum_{i,j}\bigl(\hat T_{ij}-T_{ij}\bigr)^2 .
  \label{eq:distill}
\end{equation}
Because the objective \emph{is} the retrieval metric, there is no proxy to
misalign: the deployed output is exactly the optimised quantity, not a separation
boundary or a discarded projection head. This is a metric distillation --- the
exact $D$-dimensional WJ is the teacher and the compact simplex embedding is the
student.

\noindent\textbf{Matryoshka truncation.}
We apply Eq.~\ref{eq:distill} on nested prefixes
$\{256,512,1024,2048,4096\}$ of the embedding (each $\ell_1$-renormalised onto its
own simplex), so a \emph{single} trained encoder is truncated to any dimension at
query time and the WJ stays well-defined at every width. Unlike cosine
Matryoshka~\cite{matryoshka22} (Table~\ref{tab:results}), every prefix here is
WJ-native. As Sec.~\ref{sec:fullscale} shows, the smallest prefix already
dominates, so dimension becomes a pure recall--throughput knob.

\subsection{Two-Stage Retrieval Pipeline}

\textbf{Stage~1} builds an HNSW index ($M=20$, $\text{efConstruction}=200$,
$\text{efSearch}=200$, \texttt{WeightedJaccard} space~\cite{shape2vec}) over all
$d$-dimensional embeddings using nmslib~\cite{hnsw20} and retrieves the top-$K$
candidates per query.
\textbf{Stage~2} reranks candidates by exact WJ on the original $D$-dimensional
encodings using Numba-compiled sparse WJ, recovering near-perfect recall from
the candidate pool. $K$ controls the recall--speed tradeoff
(Tables~\ref{tab:results},~\ref{tab:full}).
The decoder $g_\phi$ is discarded at inference time.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Dataset and Setup}

We evaluate on OSM Parks polygons~\cite{osm,shape2vec}, encoded by ShapeToVec into
18{,}219-dimensional simplex vectors, at two scales: a 10{,}000-polygon subset
(8{,}000 database, 2{,}000 queries) for the main results and ablations, and the
full corpus (187{,}019 database, 46{,}754 queries) for the scaling study
(Sec.~\ref{sec:fullscale}). At full scale we use a held-out 80/20 query split
(train on 80\%, evaluate on the held-out 20\%) so learned models are never
evaluated on queries seen in training. Ground-truth top-500 WJ neighbours per
query are precomputed exactly by brute force on the original $D$-dimensional
vectors.
We report Recall at $k$ (R@$k$) --- the fraction of true top-$k$ WJ neighbours
retrieved --- at $k \in \{10, 50, 500\}$, and throughput in QPS.
All models use PyTorch with AdamW ($\text{lr}{=}10^{-3}$) and cosine annealing.
QPS is measured with nmslib query threads on a single NVIDIA GPU.
% \rs{confirm Table 2 baselines (RP, InfoNCE) are evaluated on the SAME held-out
% 20\% as the distillation rows; if any used all 46,754 queries, re-eval on eval20.}

\subsection{Baselines and Ablations}

We compare: \textbf{(i)~Non-learned} --- PCA~\cite{jolliffe02},
NMF~\cite{nmf99}, Random Projection~\cite{achlioptas03} (all 512-d, $\ell_1$-projected
to $\Delta^{512}$); \textbf{(ii)~Hashing} --- ICWS~\cite{ioffe10} (512-hash WJ
sketch, brute-force$^\dagger$) and Deep Binary Hashing~\cite{deephashsurvey22}
(512-bit Hamming); \textbf{(iii)~Learned non-WJ} --- Matryoshka
MLP~\cite{matryoshka22}; and \textbf{(iv)~Ours} --- MLP cosine-triplet, AE
recon-only, MLP WJ-triplet, AE WJ-triplet+recon, and WJ-native distillation.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

We ablate each design choice (Stage-1 R@50 on the 10k subset, Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The FN filter
alone slightly weakens the 8k signal ($0.806\!\to\!0.773$): GT neighbours
dominate the small batch, leaving few true hard negatives --- an effect that
vanishes at scale. Adding WJ regression \emph{without} filtering is catastrophic
(R@10\,=\,0.510): the regression anchors positives at high WJ while corrupted
triplet gradients push in-batch false negatives below the margin; filtering
removes the conflict and restores R@50 to 0.808. Reconstruction is the decisive
component at this scale: it forces the encoder to preserve the full
input distribution, lifting R@50 from 0.806 (triplet-only) to \textbf{0.858}
(+5.0\,pp). The decoder is discarded at inference.

\noindent\textbf{Ranking objectives stop scaling.}
At $23\times$ scale the Stage-1 advantage of the learned ranking model collapses:
AE WJ-triplet+recon ties an untrained random projection on R@50 (0.656 vs.\ 0.663)
and its recall \emph{flatlines} with $k$ (R@500 $=$ R@50 $=$ 0.656), whereas
random projection's recall keeps climbing (0.722). The margin objective preserves
only the immediate neighbourhood; the broad neighbourhood is lost
(Sec.~\ref{sec:filter}). A recall-oriented InfoNCE recovers the wide-net climb
(R@500 0.727) and a more navigable index, but its near-net recall stays low
(R@50 0.606) and, like the triplet, it never beats random projection at both ends.

\noindent\textbf{Why: misalignment, not capacity.}
Recall tracks how faithfully an embedding preserves the true WJ ranking, not how
well it separates positives from negatives. \rs{Add Fig.~\ref{fig:layer}: within a
single trained ranking model, recall \emph{increases} the further one reads from
the loss --- the deployed output is the worst layer (below random projection)
while an untouched intermediate layer is best --- evidence that the contrastive
loss damages the layer it is applied to. Confirm the figure is computed on the
full corpus before final.}

\noindent\textbf{WJ-native distillation closes the gap.}
Aligning the objective with the metric (Eq.~\ref{eq:distill}) restores recall at
every $k$. On held-out queries, distillation at just 256 dimensions reaches Stage-1
R@500 $=0.886$ --- exceeding random projection (0.722) and InfoNCE (0.727) by
$16$\,pp --- while also yielding the most navigable index (2{,}492 QPS). After
exact reranking it delivers the \emph{highest} recall at the \emph{highest}
throughput at both ends of the pool: at $K{=}1000$, R@50 $=0.995$ and R@500 $=0.964$
at 1{,}030 QPS, versus random projection's 0.985/0.872 at 874 QPS; and at $K{=}2000$
its R@500 $=0.965$ exceeds random projection's 0.927 while running faster
(875 vs.\ 585 QPS). To our knowledge this is the first \emph{learned} WJ embedding
to beat random projection on \emph{both} the near- and wide-net recall--throughput
frontiers at this scale. Crucially the recall no longer flatlines (R@500 $\gg$ R@50),
confirming that the broad neighbourhood is recovered.

\noindent\textbf{Dimension is a throughput knob.}
Because the encoder is Matryoshka, one model serves all widths
(Table~\ref{tab:dim-sweep}). Recall is essentially flat from 256 to 4096
dimensions (base R@500 $0.886\!\to\!0.893$), so the smallest prefix is the natural
operating point --- a $71\times$ compression of the original 18{,}219-d vector ---
and dimension trades only throughput, not recall.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a WJ-native compression framework for efficient polygon similarity
search. At moderate scale, careful design --- a probability-simplex output
constraint, WJ triplet training with false-negative filtering, and decoder
reconstruction --- yields the highest Stage-1 recall of all ANN-compatible methods
and 78$\times$ higher throughput than brute-force ICWS, with near-perfect recall
after reranking. At full scale, however, ranking-based objectives stop beating an
untrained random projection: because the first-stage candidate generator does not
require correct order, a separation proxy is free to distort the WJ geometry that
recall depends on, and the contrastive loss damages the deployed layer itself.
\emph{WJ-native distillation} removes the misalignment by training the embedding's
Weighted Jaccard to match the true Weighted Jaccard. A single Matryoshka encoder
then beats random projection at \emph{both} near- and wide-net recall while running
faster --- the first learned WJ embedding to do so at scale --- already at a $71\times$
compression. Future work will extend the approach to billion-scale corpora and to
other set-similarity metrics that admit a simplex embedding.
